Reflections On A Freshman Year

2025-04-30

Self-Doubt And A Concussion

I’m writing to you from the patio of the SEC. The sunlight pools gently across the rolling hills of Allston, lingering in warmth and potential. The sky is a soft blue. The birds are mid-conversation. It’s a beautiful day. It’s also the last day of classes.

In this moment of stillness, I find myself looking back—over months that have moved both slowly and all at once. Just a few days ago, the Vistas students visited campus, roaming Harvard Yard with wide eyes and hesitant footsteps. I saw in them a version of myself from not so long ago: that nervous bundle of ambition, carrying that quiet weight of dreams too big to say aloud, that sense of stepping into something impossibly large.

Before this year, I had come from a place of pride. I left high school with a sense of completion—I had reformed the robotics club, brought glory to mock trial, and built community in my grade. My name carried weight in the hallways I grew up in. I felt seen. Loved. Celebrated. That sense of closure ushered in calmness. I entered the summer relaxed, indulging in the idea that I had already arrived. I had made it to Harvard.

I brought that mindset to Cambridge. I began my freshman year in a state of exploration—eager to try everything, to expand my horizons. But alongside that curiosity was something more fragile: a sense of smallness. I was suddenly surrounded by people who had started companies before they could drive, who were preparing to drop out before the semester could end. People who mastered the very subjects I merely enjoyed.

In that brilliance, I felt myself dim. I began to shrink—not out of lack, but out of doubt. Who was I to speak? To lead? To try?

I moved through the year with one foot in, one foot out. I approached clubs and classes with the mindset of a strategist rather than a student. Was this activity going to bring recognition? Would this opportunity add to some imagined legacy? Ironically, while I longed to make a name for myself, I rarely put myself out there. I didn’t join any clubs because I felt unready. I didn’t apply to any programs because I didn’t feel qualified. I didn’t talk to any people because I believed I still had so much to learn. Somewhere along the way, I stopped being myself. I convinced myself I wasn’t enough.

A Concussion

In April, that belief manifested physically. I sustained a concussion—brought on by a reckless decision to join the rugby team. Deep down, I joined the team not out of passion, but out of punishment. Some part of me felt like I needed to be hurt. Like failure had to be felt to be real. Like pain might somehow redeem my worth. I got exactly what I wanted.

So for that month of April, my brain ached. In spite of the injury, I continued to go to class, continued to go to parties, to concerts, to practices. Eventually, my condition worsened to the point that my doctors pulled me out of all activities. So for one week, I faded from daily campus life, tucked away in the dim light of my dorm.

Alone and lost in that thick fog of recovery, I discovered something unexpected. I found clarity. As I sat in my room, stirred by the day-to-day pain of a fractured brain, the noise in my head became muted. I could no longer hear the constant buzz of self-doubt or quiet comparison. What remained were the parts of me that still worked: reason, creativity, focus. In that strange stillness, I did some of my best thinking. I wrote an essay that my professor called the best he’d read in three years of teaching. I excelled on tests. I sketched ideas with renewed sharpness.

And in my isolation—on that small island of recovery—I felt a warmth I had tuned out all year. My family called. My friends reached out. In my physical lowest, I was met with steady waves of care. And for the first time in months, I let myself feel it. The support. The presence. The quiet reminder that I was never as alone as I thought.

That concussion became a turning point. It peeled back the illusion I had built around myself—that I was falling behind, that I didn’t belong, that everyone else was sprinting while I was standing still. It forced me to see that even in a state of disrepair, I could still meet the moment. Still thrive. Still be seen.

The limits I had felt all year were never real. They were constructs of my own mind. And now, finally, I’m ready to let them go.

A Commitment To Change

The weekend after I recovered, I found myself Googling what “black tie” meant. Fête was around the corner, and I had no idea how to dress. Somewhere between etiquette guides and formalwear blogs, I stumbled upon an article titled How To Thrive At Harvard: Advice From An Alumnus.

The first piece of advice was pertinent to my search: buy a tuxedo.

The second suggestion however, made me pause:

Ask how Harvard has changed you. Then ask how you’ve changed Harvard.

The first question came easily. Harvard has changed me. I’ve made real friends, encountered real challenges, and begun the patient process of discovering who I am becoming. I’ve learned to dream wider. To live with more questions.

But the second question—how have I changed Harvard?—that one made me sit in quiet contemplation.

I haven’t. Not yet. Not even close. This year, I’ve been a quiet observer, orbiting the center of things without ever stepping fully in. I kept looking outward, clinging to the belief that the “real world” existed somewhere beyond campus. That true success would be not from doing the cushioned activities of a college club, but by making strides in the outside world.

In this belief, I failed to even position myself to answer such a question. I didn’t join any clubs. I didn’t step into any campus groups. I told myself I was waiting—but for what, I wasn’t sure.

Only now, after a year of college, do I realize that Harvard isn’t a pause. It isn’t a waiting room. It’s a platform—a concentrated pocket of reality. An institution older than the country itself, built to crystallize ideas that endure far beyond our time here. To overlook that is to waste one of the rarest opportunities we’ll ever be given. To fail to seize it would be my ultimate regret.

And so, as I look ahead, I want to show up differently. No more quiet Kevin. No more hesitating Kevin.

I want to help shape the engineering culture at Harvard. I want to help build something rigorous and real—where builders don’t just build with logic, but with care. Where engineering becomes a force for elevation, not just efficiency. I want to help define what it means to be a Harvard engineer, and wear that title with pride.

I also want to lean into the interdisciplinary spirit that pulses beneath the surface of this place. I want to sit at tables where physics meets poetry, where machine learning brushes up against metaphysics. I want to live at the crossroads, because I believe that meaning lives there too. You don’t need to fit into a single box. You can be many things, and still be whole.

And most of all, I want to shape myself. To move from floating to grounded, from thinking to doing. I want to define a personal philosophy that holds me steady. I want to find something worth throwing myself at—completely, without apology. A purpose that draws out my fullest self. And when I find it, I want to share it. To spark something in others. To help create a culture of contribution, one built not on prestige, but on presence. Not on ego, but on care.

So as I write this, as I wrap up my school year, I end not with closure, but with a beginning.
I feel stirred. Restless. Ready.

I’ve arrived—but now, I choose to begin.

I will leap. I will commit. I will create. I will give more than I take. I will stop waiting for permission to be who I already am.

Harvard will not be something I pass through quietly. It will be something I shape with the full force of my presence. And perhaps—if I’m lucky—it will shape me just the same.

So I ask myself:

What will I build with all that I am?

Who will I shape, and be shaped by in return?

And when I’m gone—

What whisper of me will echo permanently through time?
